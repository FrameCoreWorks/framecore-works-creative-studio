# Music-video direction method

This reference turns a user's song context and associations into a visual thesis without pretending there is one objectively correct interpretation. Use it with `SKILL.md`; do not turn it into a mandatory form.

## 1. Establish evidence and intent

Keep three layers visibly distinct in the working brief:

- **Provided facts:** song/audio actually available, artist or subject details the user supplied, approved lyrics, declared audience/format, references and constraints.
- **Observed or verified material:** musical qualities or structure the active system actually analysed, plus public context established through the required research preflight. Name the method/source when it affects the direction.
- **Creative interpretation:** proposed emotional reading, image logic, relationship between sound and action, and assumptions. A title, genre label, lyric fragment or artist name alone does not prove a tempo, section map, intention or biography.

When audio is absent or not analysed, work from the user's description and call the concept provisional where musical specifics matter. Ask for audio, a section map or a more useful description only if the missing evidence changes the requested direction. Do not fill gaps with invented timecodes. If the user wants immediate ideation, proceed with relative or conceptual mapping and label it.

## 2. Find the film's engine

Identify what can carry the experience: artist presence, a visible choice/action, a narrative thread, an image system, a fashion/persona statement, a rhythmic relationship, or a deliberate blend. Select the primary engine and assign secondary layers a supporting job. Relevant forms include:

- performance-led: screen presence, gaze, gesture and camera relation carry the film;
- narrative: events carry a story or changing situation, without assuming literal lyric illustration;
- symbolic/abstract: an image logic or atmosphere carries meaning, with internal coherence;
- hybrid: performance, narrative and symbolic layers have defined roles;
- persona/fashion-led: silhouette, styling and presence are the content;
- rhythm-led: cut, motion, hold or deliberate anti-sync respond to the music.

These are useful lenses, not a closed taxonomy. A small or unusual form may fit better. Choose from what the user cares about, what the song material supports and what a viewer can perceive. Do not treat genre as a preset.

## 3. Translate song into image

Do not ask only “what does the lyric depict?” Ask what kind of image/action relationship the brief wants:

- **Emotional:** a visible behavior or physical state carries the feeling, even when it contrasts with the words.
- **Atmospheric:** space, light, material, distance and duration make a world the song inhabits.
- **Narrative:** an event or relationship unfolds alongside, against, or through the song.
- **Rhythmic:** movement, edit, camera, stillness or accents relate to supplied or verified musical information.
- **Symbolic:** a coherent image rule carries meaning without explaining it literally.
- **Counterpoint:** the picture deliberately resists or contradicts a musical/lyrical expectation for a stated viewer effect.

Several can coexist. Avoid translating every word into an object or changing every section into a new visual. State why the selected relationship belongs to this song and persona. If lyrics are quoted, keep to the user's supplied text and only quote the amount needed.

## 4. Make a world with a few usable rules

Define only world decisions that anchor the direction: reality level; spatial continuity or fragmentation; temporal behavior if non-real-time; active versus passive environment; light and material behavior; palette or contrast rule; and what the camera may reveal or withhold. Each rule must serve the thesis. One restrained, coherent world can be more distinctive than a catalogue of locations and effects.

Every large visual element needs a function: communicate the song-image relation, alter a viewer's reading, support a performance choice, or create a useful rhythmic event. Remove decoration that has no relationship to the artist, song or brief. Do not promise visual effects or physical transformations as if they already exist.

## 5. Separate emotion from energy

Energy concerns perceived force, pace or density; emotional experience concerns what the viewer feels, attends to or understands. They may align, diverge or remain steady. Identify an opening experience and the intended later state only when the concept has a progression. A sustained, hypnotic, funny, observational, confrontational or deliberately unresolved experience is valid if its attention logic holds.

If proposing a change, identify its cause and perceptible effect. The chorus may release, withhold, repeat, reframe or preserve the visual state. A bridge may break pattern or hold it. Do not write “escalates to a cathartic finale” unless the brief and chosen direction earn that promise.

## 6. Explore without template drift

When alternatives are useful, vary the creative engine, viewer relation or world rule—not merely palette, weather or camera adjectives. A quick concept should be assessable from its thesis, one concrete image/action and the way it differs. After a route is selected, preserve its useful decisions; do not restart all concepts during each revision.

A practical thesis test:

1. Can the director explain what the image does in relation to the specific song or artist?
2. Can the viewer encounter the intended effect through visible choices without needing hidden backstory?
3. Can a downstream collaborator tell which decisions are locked, approximate or open?
4. If the concept's key device is removed, does the experience meaningfully change? If not, it may be decoration rather than the engine.

Use the test to locate gaps, not as a scorecard requiring a particular style or emotional arc.
