# Mascots and stylized characters

For a drawn or modelled character that a brand, school or product will reuse: a mascot, a stylized person, an animal or object with a face. The goal is a character that reads at small sizes, stays the same across generators, artists and media, and can be produced. Real people follow the [human identity workbook](human-identity-workbook.md) instead.

## Design for reuse

- **Silhouette first.** Fill the character black: it should still be recognisable, and the head, ears, tail, hat or tool should not merge into the body. Test the silhouette at the smallest planned size (a favicon, an embroidered patch, a sticker).
- **Shape language.** Pick one dominant shape (round reads friendly and safe, square stable and strong, triangular fast or sharp) and use the others only as accents. State it, so later artists keep it.
- **Proportions.** Fix the head-to-body ratio (a mascot for children usually 1:1 to 1:2, a brand character 1:2.5 to 1:4) and the eye size and spacing; these are the first things generators drift on.
- **Few, flat features.** Three to five identity anchors (for example: one ear folded, a scarf in the accent colour, a white belly patch) beat many details; small textures and gradients disappear in print, embroidery and animation.
- **Colours.** Three to five flat colours from the brand palette with HEX values, plus an outline colour or a no-outline rule; one-colour and reversed versions for stamps, embroidery and dark grounds.
- **Age and audience.** For children: no sharp teeth, no frightening eyes, no sexualised poses or adult products; a friendly expression readable without words.
- **Not someone else's.** No resemblance to an existing licensed character or a competitor's mascot; similarity is a risk to report, and clearance is a legal check, not a design one.

## The character sheet

Deliver the [mascot sheet](../assets/mascot-sheet.template.md) before any campaign images:

1. **Turnaround:** front, three-quarter, side and back in the same pose and scale, on a neutral ground.
2. **Expressions:** the six or eight the brand will use (neutral, happy, surprised, thinking, sad, proud), mouth and eyebrow shapes drawn consistently.
3. **Poses and gestures:** the few actions the character performs (waving, pointing, holding the product), with hand count and shape fixed.
4. **Colour and construction:** HEX swatches, outline rule, proportions and the anchor list in words.
5. **Do and do not:** stretched, recoloured, wrong number of fingers, extra accessories, off-brand emotions.
6. **Small sizes and one colour:** the silhouette and a one-colour version at the smallest planned size.

## Keeping it the same across tools

- Generate or draw the turnaround first and approve it; every later image uses it as a reference with the role "match identity", and the anchors as a fixed sentence repeated word for word in every prompt.
- With a generator that supports character or element references (for example Kling Elements or reference images in Veo, Runway or Seedance), bind the approved sheet; without it, expect drift and plan a review pass per image.
- For animation, a vector or 3D master gives exact repeats; a raster from an image generator is a concept, not a production master (see the [brand identity profile](../../workflow-orchestrator/references/brand-identity-workflow.md)).
- Review each new image against the sheet: silhouette, proportions, anchors, colours, hand count, expression. Repair one property at a time.

## Delivery

State what each file is: concept raster, approved sheet, vector or 3D master. A mascot used as a trademark needs its own clearance and, if an artist drew it, a written transfer of rights. For a mascot costume, give the maker the turnaround with real measurements and the materials' colours as fabric samples, not HEX values alone.
